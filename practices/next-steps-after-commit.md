## Rule
Any reply that made a commit ends with an explicit **Next Steps** statement addressed to the person: what, if anything, they need to do now.

**It is set off so it cannot be skimmed past.** A REAL MARKDOWN HEADING at the end of the reply -- `## Next Steps` or wording that plainly says the same -- with the actions themselves in bold. **A bold line is not a heading**: a reply that closes on `**What I still need from you:**` has not done this, because bold text is exactly what the rest of the reply is full of and it does not stand out from it. Said literally on 2026-09-08, after a reply used a bold lead-in and the reader had to go looking for the list anyway. A reply that reports a lot of work is exactly the reply whose last paragraph gets skimmed, and this is the one part of it that is addressed to the person rather than about the work.

**An item that asks for a decision says what the decision is.** Naming the thing is not enough: an item like *"Decide on `universal-code-cites-team-slug`"* uses a slug or a filename the reader has never opened, and nobody can act on a word they do not recognize. Every item asking for a decision adds a **one-sentence summary of the decision itself, in parentheses**, after the name -- *"Decide whether `fail-gracefully` moves to the universal catalogue (five files in the public engine cite a rule nobody outside the team set can look up)"*. The name still appears, so the thing can be found; the parenthetical is added, never substituted for it. This applies to a decision, an approval, or a question -- anything whose subject is an identifier rather than a plain description. It does not apply to a plainly-worded action that already says what it is: *"Delete the five branches below"* needs no gloss.

**And every item carries the session's own RECOMMENDATION, in the item, not somewhere above it.** Not only what the decision is -- what the session thinks should be done about it, and in one clause, why. *"Close both rather than merge (they are behind `main`, and merging would resurrect a rule that was promoted to universal)"*, not *"Decide what to do with the two branches"*. **A list that hands over the questions and keeps the answers has moved the work rather than done it**, and it is the same failure as naming an unread slug: the reader has to go back up through the reply -- or worse, into the repository -- to reconstruct a view the session already formed. Morgan, 2026-09-08, asking for this to be made stronger after a reply gave him four items and no recommendations: *"give me these lists always with the one sentence summary of the issue and your recommendation so I don't need to find it"*. If the session genuinely has no view, it says that in the item and says what it would need in order to have one -- that is a recommendation too, and it is not the same as silence.

**Every branch, merge or push names its repository, in parentheses.** Several repositories are routinely attached at once, and "merged into `main`" is then a sentence nobody can act on -- `main` in which one? Any mention of a merge, a push, a branch or a commit that reaches the person says which repository it happened in, right there: *"merged into `main` (precedent-team-repo-maintenance)"*, *"pushed to `precedent-beta-v01` (BestPractice)"*. This is not satisfied by having said it earlier in the reply or by a link the repository name can be read out of -- the name goes in the sentence, because the sentence is what gets read. Raised by Morgan 2026-09-07, from replies that said "merged to main" in a session holding four checkouts.

**It survives a tangent.** If the person replies with a question, an objection, or a change of subject, the next reply answers that -- and then restates the outstanding Next Steps anyway, in full, even though they have not changed and were stated a moment ago. An action not yet taken is still outstanding no matter how many turns have passed since it was raised, and the turn where the subject changed is precisely the turn where it falls off. Repeating it costs a few lines; not repeating it is how work sits unmerged. This applies to every reply in the thread while something remains outstanding, not only to replies that themselves committed something. This holds even when the reply already asked a question or mentioned an action item earlier in its body: restate it at the end anyway, because the end is the part least likely to be missed.

**When there is nothing, say there is nothing -- explicitly, in those words.** "There is nothing you need to do now", or wording that plainly says the same thing, as its own closing statement. Never silence, and never dropping the section on the grounds that it would be empty. An absent Next Steps and a Next Steps saying nothing is outstanding look identical on the page and mean opposite things, and telling which one you are looking at means re-reading the whole reply -- which is the exact cost this practice exists to remove. This half was in the rule before 2026-09-07 and was being missed, because it sat as one sentence inside the tangent clause above rather than standing on its own; it is its own clause now for the same reason the closing statement itself is set off.

**And the closing statement ends by saying whether the SESSION can be archived, in one of two fixed sentences.** *"You can archive this session."* -- only when nothing outstanding needs this session (everything left is the person's to do somewhere else, or there is nothing at all) **and** all three archive conditions hold:

1. **The work is safe somewhere that isn't this session's container** -- pushed, published, or deliberately abandoned. Archiving releases the session's container and its filesystem goes with it; unarchiving provisions a fresh one. Anything uncommitted, or committed but unpushed, is gone for good. "Pushed" is the common case, not the only one: work published somewhere outside the container -- an artifact, a sent file -- is already safe the moment it publishes, with no separate push step to wait for.
2. **There is nothing left to do directly in this session** -- its pull request merged or closed, any open question answered, nothing queued to push, and no other next action that has to happen in this specific window. A non-blocking loose end does not fail this condition: a suggestion that is minor or a separate matter, a question that doesn't change what happens next either way, something the reply says it is waiting on that won't change the result. Name it and let the person decide.
3. **No scheduled job is bound to this session.** A Routine created with `persistent_session_id` fires INTO one named session, and an archived session accepts no events, so archiving its target is how a scheduled wake silently stops arriving. Check `list_triggers` before saying the sentence. A Routine that spawns a fresh session on each firing is unaffected.

Otherwise: *"Don't archive this session until ..."*, naming what -- whether that is something still on the person (a question the reply asked, a decision it needs before it can carry on) or a reason the session itself is not archivable yet (a Routine is bound to it, it holds an open pull request, its work is not pushed). Either way the sentence names the actual reason, because "don't archive it" with nothing after it is exactly the line nobody can act on. The distinction is the useful half. **Most items do not need the session open at all** -- deleting a merged branch, reviewing a pull request, approving something in another window -- and a list of those reads exactly like a list of things somebody has to come back here for. The fixed wording is not fussiness: it is what makes the line checkable, and an absent line and a "nothing is outstanding" line look identical on the page, which is the same failure the clause above this one exists to remove. Morgan, 2026-09-13, on the original wording: *"that final message should also explicitly say 'You can close this session' or 'Don't close this session until you do the above items'"* -- revised 2026-09-14 to speak in archive terms, since archiving the session (not closing a tab) is the action actually taken now, and to put the three conditions above in front of the "can" branch rather than the weaker bar of "nothing is outstanding", so this line stops being a green light to archive something a bound Routine or an open pull request still needs.

**Every session this reply spawned or woke is listed there too, with its link.** One line each: which repo it is rooted in, what it was asked to do, and the `https://claude.ai/code/<session id>` link -- the same link the reply already gave near the top, repeated at the end where it will actually be found. This covers a session the person asked for and a session the reply decided to create on its own; both are things running in their name that they cannot see from this window, and a link buried in the middle of a long reply is a session nobody can keep an eye on. Morgan, 2026-09-14, after exactly that: *"your link to the session you seeded was lost in your sea of information"*. It is not a Next Step in the sense of something to do -- nothing may be required at all -- so it goes in the section as its own line, and the closing session-disposition sentence still says only whether THIS session can be archived.

That closing statement also names any branch, in any repo touched during the session, that the branch-cleanup method calls safe to delete: a branch whose most recent PR merged, or whose most recent PR closed without merging because a later PR superseded it (called out as such, separately from the merged ones) -- identified by who opened or drove that PR (a GitHub login, not git commit authorship, since a Claude Code commit is authored as `Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>` regardless of who is running the session), and never a branch created by someone else, the default branch, or the branch the session itself is working on. A branch with an open PR, or no PR at all, is left alone and does not need naming here. Group the list by repo when more than one is touched; say plainly that there is nothing to flag when the method turns up no candidates.

Whenever the session itself merges a pull request, that closing line also names the branch that PR merged from, says it is now safe to delete, and gives a direct, clickable link to the merged PR's own page (e.g. `https://github.com/<owner>/<repo>/pull/<number>`) -- GitHub shows a one-click **Delete branch** button right there once a PR is merged, so the link is what turns "safe to delete" into something actionable without hunting for the page. Do this for every branch the session merges in that session, not just the last one, across whichever repos the session touched. This is narrower than the branch-cleanup method above: it fires only for a PR the session itself merged in that same session. Naming a branch this way is never license to also scan for other, older merged branches beyond that -- retroactively hunting down every stale merged branch in a repo is a separate, one-off task, done only when asked for directly, not something this standing practice performs on its own.

## Detail
**The restate-after-a-tangent half was added 2026-09-07, and it was added because of an incident this practice's original wording did not prevent.** The rule as first written fires on *a reply that made a commit*. A reply that answers a question and commits nothing did not trigger it, so a thread that drifted -- a question, an answer, another question -- could carry an outstanding "you need to merge this" from three turns back and never say so again. That is what happened to `claude/pre-launch-audit-fixes-7wumzx`: real fixes across three repos, reported once, never restated, never merged, and rediscovered from scratch by a later session that filed two of them as fresh findings.

This is not a request for a summary of what changed -- that is the commit message and diff's job. It is specifically about the person's follow-on actions: approve something, review a PR, run a command, answer a question the reply raised, wait on something else, or nothing at all. A reply with no commit in it does not need this -- it is the commit that makes the omission costly, since a commit is exactly the kind of action whose loose ends (a push still pending, a PR still to open, a check still red) are easy to bury in a long reply and easy to miss.

The branch-deletion note piggybacks on the same closing line for the same reason: a merged or superseded branch left lying around is exactly the kind of loose end this practice already exists to surface, and nobody is more likely to go looking for it unprompted than for any other follow-on action. It is a recommendation, never an action taken on its own -- nothing gets deleted without confirmation.

The session-merge case gets the extra link because the session already has everything the link needs -- it just performed that exact merge, so it knows the PR's URL without looking anything up. That is also why it stays scoped to that session's own merges rather than growing into a general branch audit: a broader sweep for other old merged branches sitting around a repo is real, separate work, with its own judgment calls about what is actually safe, and folding it silently into every closing line would turn a cheap habit into an open-ended chore.

**The three archive conditions are written out in the Rule above on purpose, and that was a decision rather than an oversight.** They were defined by a practice in one person's individual set ([spec/CLOSING_SECTION_TO_UNIVERSAL.md](https://github.com/alex137/BestPractice/blob/staging/spec/CLOSING_SECTION_TO_UNIVERSAL.md) names the entanglement). A universal rule demanding a fixed sentence whose meaning is defined privately is a rule most adopters cannot apply, so the conditions travel with the sentence. The individual practice keeps its own fuller treatment -- what archiving costs, what it does not buy back, when it is the wrong call -- and nothing here contradicts it; an adopter who wants only the mechanical bar has it here.

## Why
Long replies bury the important trailing detail -- a question that was asked, a step flagged as still needed -- even when it was stated clearly earlier in the same message. Restating it as a short, explicit closing line costs little and means nobody has to re-read a whole reply to find out whether anything is still on them. The same is true of a branch that has done its job: nobody deletes it unless someone says it is safe to, and a direct link to the one page that already has the delete button saves finding that page by hand.

## Install
**Mechanically enforced since 2026-09-13, and what stopped it before was a false premise.** This section used to say there was no check to write: that the rule governs how a reply gets *written*, that no artifact distinguishes a correct closing from a missing one, and that the one place the omission is visible is the conversation transcript, "which a repo-scoped check script has no access to". The last clause is the one that was wrong. Claude Code hands its Stop hook a JSON payload carrying `transcript_path`, and that file is the session's own log -- final assistant message included -- on disk, before the hook runs. A Stop hook that exits 2 sends its stderr back to the model and the turn continues, so a reply that broke this rule gets rewritten before anyone sees it.

The mechanism is [tools/precedent_reply_check.py](https://github.com/alex137/BestPractice/blob/staging/tools/precedent_reply_check.py); the requirement is this repository's own [reply_check.json](https://github.com/alex137/BestPractice/blob/staging/reply_check.json), which names the heading pattern and the two closing sentences. **Nothing to install: an adopter running Precedent gets this declaration with the engine.** That is a real, opinionated default and it is deliberate -- a universal practice nobody enforces is the failure this project spent a week fixing at the other end of the engine. There is no per-adopter wording override yet; an adopter who wants different phrases edits this repository's declaration, and a proper `overrides` key on a declaration is real follow-up work nobody has built.

**What it checks, and what it still cannot.** It checks the two things with fixed shapes: a real markdown heading matching /next step/i, and one of the two session-disposition sentences. It cannot check the parts that need judgment -- whether a decision item carries its one-sentence summary, whether every item carries the session's recommendation, whether a branch or merge named its repository, whether outstanding items were restated after a tangent. Those stay a rule a session has to apply. What the check buys is that the section always EXISTS, which is what makes everything missing from it visible.

Why it was worth building rather than living with: Morgan, 2026-09-13 -- *"I feel like it sometimes does that, sometimes doesn't, how can we force that?"* The same diagnosis found that the reply gate had never served a private set at all. [precedent_gate.py](https://github.com/alex137/BestPractice/blob/staging/tools/precedent_gate.py) read one directory, the consuming repo's own `practices/`, so this practice's `gates: ["reply"]` registration had no invocation point anywhere, and the only channel left was the occasion index -- which fires when a session recognises the occasion, which is exactly the "sometimes" he was describing. Fixed the same day in the engine (`resolved_gate_practices`).

**It carries no `occasion:` line, and that is the routing rather than an omission.** The `reply` gate loads it at the end of every turn and the stop hook refuses the turn outright, so the occasion index -- which fires only when a session RECOGNISES the occasion -- is the one channel this practice does not need. Dropped 2026-09-14, on [build_views.py](https://github.com/alex137/BestPractice/blob/staging/tools/build_views.py)'s own reduction menu, when landing it here put the generated index 33 tokens over its ceiling. Step 1 of that menu is exactly this case (a `**` practice that declares a gate); raising the ceiling is step 3 and was not taken. No other practice's line was touched.
